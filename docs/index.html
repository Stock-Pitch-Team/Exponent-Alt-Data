Thesis trigger 1 — “growth from volume + rate, not rate alone”

Workforce: is Exponent hiring again?

Exponent bills by the expert-hour, so consultant headcount IS capacity. The company over-hired in 2022–23, cut back in 2024, and the buy case needs headcount growing again. We track it independently, using the firm's own website and twenty years of web archives.

Thesis trigger 2 — the litigation moat is intact and demand is growing

Courtroom: the franchise, measured in court records

Roughly 60% of revenue is reactive work anchored in disputes and litigation. Court records are public — so instead of trusting the “go-to expert firm” reputation, we counted it.

Thesis trigger 3 — the reactive pipeline is filling

Failure demand: what breaks today is billed tomorrow

Exponent gets hired when products catch fire, get recalled, or hurt someone. US regulators publish every recall, complaint and adverse event — a real-time gauge of the demand environment, months to years before it can show up in Exponent's revenue.

Thesis trigger 4 — the scientific moat is being maintained

Science moat: the credential engine

Exponent's pricing power rests on ~1,000 consultants, most with doctorates, whose credibility survives cross-examination. Their publication record — and the blue-chip corporations that co-author with them — is public and countable.

The bear case, taken seriously — can AI do this work?

AI exposure: the threat, measured in filings rather than opinions

The obvious question about a firm of 996 scientists is whether software is coming for their billable hours. No public dataset honestly scores that, and we won't invent one. So we asked sources that can answer: Exponent's own annual reports, where a misleading statement carries legal consequence, and our own court records — because testimony given under Rule 702 requires a human who can be cross-examined.

Credibility anchor + risk sensor

Government: contracts and rulemaking

Two smaller but fully-verifiable signals: federal agencies hiring Exponent directly (a quality stamp), and Exponent's studies appearing inside federal rulemaking — where both the firm's regulatory franchise and the “hired gun” criticism of it are visible in the same data.

Thesis trigger 5 — client R&D budgets are recovering

Clients & financials: the demand pool and the ground truth

Exponent never names clients. We reconstructed a documented sample of them from co-authored research and court records, then pulled each one's R&D budget from its SEC filings — because when client R&D grows, the proactive consulting that ~40% of Exponent's revenue depends on grows with it.

Relative framing — is Exponent winning its niche?

Peers: the same lenses, pointed at the competition

Absolute numbers can always be argued with; relative ones are harder. The same pipelines used above, run against the closest listed consulting peers — FTI Consulting (FCN), CRA International (CRAI) and ICF (ICFI) — on scientific output, profitability, and courtroom presence.

Methodology, sources & honest limitations

Every chart above carries its own “How to read this chart” explainer and a provenance footer. This section shows the exact rules the pipelines used, the status of every dataset, and the full list of caveats — including the ones that cut against the thesis.

The rules of this dashboard

  • Real data only. Every number originates from a public primary source (CourtListener, SEC EDGAR, NHTSA, CPSC, openFDA, USAspending, Regulations.gov, OpenAlex, the Internet Archive, exponent.com). If a source failed, the page shows a grey “data unavailable” card — a gap is never filled with an estimate.
  • Normalize before you conclude. Archives grow and portals shrink; wherever a raw count could mislead, the chart shows a rate against the source's own total volume.
  • Small samples say so. Anything computed from a few dozen records (like the Daubert analysis) is labeled with its sample size.
  • Verifiability. Evidence tables link every underlying record so any claim can be checked in the primary source.
